# Controlled sibling exposure and locked cross-source reliability in onion image classification

## Abstract

Related image files can inflate evaluation scores, but ordinary file-versus-group comparisons also change test composition and training support. We audited two public bulb-onion (Allium cepa L.) collections and separated controlled sibling exposure from source-locked transfer. In augmented COLD, 4,502 files formed 816 filename families. A 30-repeat replacement experiment fixed 163 probe images, 82 never-exposed sentinel images and a class-matched training budget of 1,142 files per repetition. Replacing training families with probe-family siblings raised four-class ResNet18 balanced accuracy from 0.642 to 0.795 on probes, while sentinel performance changed from 0.613 to 0.605. The mean probe-minus-sentinel change was 0.162, with a split-seed percentile range of −0.014–0.355. The corresponding binary probe gain was smaller, 0.038, showing that the leakage effect depends on the endpoint. In a separate binary transfer experiment, selection, calibration and thresholding used 1,643 TOM2024 images grouped by 103 acquisition days. Balanced accuracy fell from 0.927 internally to 0.655 on 813 source-disjoint COLD images; the strongest simple baseline reached 0.571. Brier score worsened from 0.049 to 0.240. A source-only calibration-construction sensitivity reduced external Brier score to 0.231 without restoring decision performance. Newer frozen backbones also failed to preserve the source operating point. Controlled sibling familiarity and cross-source decision reliability are distinct problems: neither file uniqueness nor good internal ranking establishes transportability. The added controls are post hoc analyses of existing archives, not new biological validation.

**Keywords:** agricultural image classification; data leakage; domain shift; calibration; grouped validation; *Allium cepa*

## 1 Introduction

Deep image classifiers can separate plant-health categories with high apparent accuracy, especially when training and test images come from the same curated collection, as illustrated by the widely used PlantVillage resource \cite{Hughes2015PlantVillage}. The practical inference is harder: whether a model will retain useful discrimination, probability calibration, and decision behavior when it encounters a new acquisition process. Controlled backgrounds, recurring specimens, camera pipelines, class-specific collection sessions, and pre-generated augmentation can all create correlations that a file-random split does not respect \cite{Shorten2019AugmentationSurvey}. A model can then recognize source context or a transformed sibling rather than learn a signal that transports to new plants and new image sources. The distinction matters because a high internal score is often interpreted as evidence for an agricultural diagnostic system, although the evaluation may support only within-archive file discrimination.

Plant-disease research has documented this gap from several directions. Early transfer-learning studies showed that performance on laboratory-style repositories need not survive more heterogeneous images \cite{Mohanty2016PlantDisease,Barbedo2018Factors,Arsenovic2019Limitations}. PlantDoc was created partly to expose models to less controlled field photographs \cite{Singh2020PlantDoc}. More recently, Xu et al. argued that useful plant-disease datasets need explicit acquisition, temporal, spatial, and annotation metadata, together with test sets designed around the intended challenge rather than a convenient random partition \cite{Xu2024PlantDiseaseDatasets}. General machine-learning work likewise identifies leakage as a reproducibility problem, while agricultural augmentation studies show that transformations generated before splitting can produce severe optimism \cite{Kapoor2023Leakage,Gerz2026AugmentationLeakage}. These concerns are methodological, but they have direct image-processing consequences: they determine whether reported features and classifiers recognize plant appearance across sources or reproduce correlations already present in an archive.

Onion imagery offers a useful case. Prior work ranges from multispectral downy-mildew assessment to multi-crop archives with onion categories, a dedicated chilli-and-onion leaf dataset, and a recent onion detector acquired in a natural production setting \cite{Kim2020OnionDownyMildew,Appiah2025TOM2024,Aishwarya2024COLD,Raj2025YOLOODD}. Yet the public archives differ in label ontology, capture process, class balance, and derivative-image structure. Their folder names are dataset assignments, not independent laboratory confirmation of a causal agent. This study therefore addresses the binary and observable endpoint **healthy versus dataset-labelled visibly affected foliage**. It does not attempt pathogen diagnosis, and it does not merge disease names across datasets as though they formed a validated pathology ontology.

An image file is not automatically an independent observation. Augmentation families, acquisition days and dataset sources describe different dependencies, and a split must address the dependency relevant to its intended claim. These units are not necessarily nested, nor is an external dataset necessarily harder than an internal grouped split. Here, augmented-COLD four-class classification and TOM-to-COLD binary transfer are separate experiments. Their scores cannot be joined into a single degradation curve. The question is whether evidence remains credible when known shared information is controlled, and whether the resulting decision pipeline transports across sources.

Recent work in *Pattern Analysis and Applications* places this problem at the intersection of leakage control, cross-domain pattern recognition, and agricultural image analysis. The closest direct precedent is Menco-Tovar et al., who compared region-of-interest splitting with original-image-controlled splitting in bacterial microscopy and found Macro-F1 inflation of up to 0.0375 when regions from the same image could cross subsets \cite{MencoTovar2026OriginalImage}. We therefore do not claim that grouping derivatives by their source image is new. Apicella et al. have already examined test-sample contamination over a range of leakage proportions, and Guignard et al. have derived leakage properties for clustered observations \cite{Apicella2025Leakage,Guignard2024ClusterLeakage}. Our question is narrower: does exposure to non-identical augmented siblings change predictions on fixed test images when training file counts, class counts and family counts are held constant? A separate, never-exposed sentinel set tests whether the change is concentrated among families with training siblings. A second experiment evaluates discrimination, probability quality and a frozen operating point across image sources. Roselló et al. introduced Kurcuma, a seven-dataset kitchen-utensil collection and baseline for unsupervised domain adaptation, making the relationship between training and application distributions an explicit part of the recognition problem \cite{Rosello2023Kurcuma}. Gilo et al. combined correlation alignment and entropy minimization for subdomain adaptation, while Chouhan et al. developed masked adversarial adaptation for semantic segmentation \cite{Gilo2024Subdomain,Chouhan2025MDAAF}. Gupta et al. addressed imbalanced fruit-disease classification with a pre-trained noise-based unsupervised GAN \cite{Gupta2025FruitGAN}. Mitchell et al. studied image classifiers under spurious correlations without requiring group labels, while Gebler et al. experimentally evaluated an event-based stereo dataset for insect segmentation \cite{Mitchell2025Spurious,Gebler2026StereoInsect}. Together, these studies establish dependence control, domain change, spurious association, dataset evaluation, and agricultural recognition as active pattern-analysis questions. The present work asks whether the source split, calibration procedure, and locked decision rule provide independent evidence of transport before target-domain adaptation is attempted.

We reconstruct augmentation lineage, retain the original file-versus-family comparison as a descriptive split sensitivity, and add a controlled family-replacement experiment with fixed probe and sentinel tests. We then examine source-locked transfer with simple baselines and frozen deep features, including a diagnostic of the calibration score construction. The added experiments were designed after the original results were known; their protocols and input hashes were frozen before their own fitting runs. The contribution is an empirical separation of dependence-related evaluation gains from cross-source probability and decision failure. The reporting checklist summarizes established validation safeguards; it is not presented as a new theory or algorithm.

## 2 Materials and methods

### 2.1 Study design and endpoint

Figure 1 separates the original transfer study from the augmented-archive experiments and identifies the information available to each analysis. TOM2024 supplied the development domain; raw COLD images supplied the external domain; the fixed augmented COLD archive was reserved for a separate split-unit experiment. The main endpoint mapped the TOM2024 labels Alternaria, Fusarium, and virosis to visibly affected foliage and retained its healthy label as the negative class. COLD Iris yellow spot virus, purple blotch, and pooled Stemphylium/Colletotrichum leaf-blight labels were mapped to visibly affected foliage, with COLD healthy as negative. The mapping harmonizes visual status only. It does not assert equivalence between named disorders, verify causal organisms, or support treatment advice.

All analyzed crop images were of bulb onion, *Allium cepa*. They should not be described as Welsh onion or bunching onion (*A. fistulosum*). Exact-content hashes, archive names, source keys, inclusion decisions, and model inputs were recorded in machine-readable manifests. Raw public images were retained at their source repositories; derived manifests, predictions, folds, metrics, and hashes provide the reproducible analysis trail.

### 2.2 Data provenance and source audit

Table 1 summarizes the distinction between the distributed file count, exact-unique files, recoverable source or acquisition groups, and the inference supported by each resource.

**TOM2024.** The Category A archive contained 3,044 onion files \cite{Appiah2025TOM2024}. A 13-digit identifier embedded in every filename was parsed as the raw-photo timestamp key. The 3,044 files collapsed to 2,421 source groups. Nineteen groups contained conflicting folder labels and were excluded rather than assigned by majority vote. We then kept the highest-resolution representative from each remaining group, leaving 2,402 conflict-free representatives. Caterpillar images were excluded because an insect-presence category changes the target construct, and bulb-blight images were excluded because the visible organ and class support did not match the foliar endpoint. The eligible foliar set contained 1,647 representatives. Four redundant exact-content files were removed at analysis time, producing 1,643 unique development images from 103 UTC acquisition days: 459 Alternaria, 595 Fusarium, 167 virosis, and 422 healthy.

Labels retained measurable day structure: normalized mutual information was 0.282 for day versus the original multiclass label and 0.113 for day versus the binary endpoint; 46 of 103 days contained only one binary state. A day is not a verified plant or plot identity, but it is a coarser barrier against repeated acquisition context where plant identifiers are unavailable.

**COLD raw.** The public machine-readable COLD snapshot contained 815 raw files, whereas the accompanying data article reports 864 images \cite{Aishwarya2024COLD}. We report the exact analyzed snapshot rather than substituting the article total. Two within-class duplicate pairs reduced the archive to 813 unique images: 426 healthy, 279 Iris yellow spot virus, 90 pooled Stemphylium/Colletotrichum leaf blight, and 18 purple blotch. No exact hash or source key overlapped TOM2024. COLD filenames do not expose plant identity, plot, or acquisition day. Consequently, it is an external dataset test but not an estimate based on known biological replicates.

**COLD augmented.** Every one of 4,502 augmented filenames followed the pattern `dr_<parent index>_<random suffix>.jpg`. The parent index provided a complete, directly observed grouping of the augmented files into 816 filename families. All augmented files had unique SHA-256 and perceptual hashes, so exact deduplication alone could not reveal their dependence. Family counts were 426 healthy, 282 Iris yellow spot virus, 90 pooled leaf blight, and 18 purple blotch. The corresponding file counts were 1,278, 1,272, 1,217, and 735. Thus, purple blotch had 40.8 distributed files per family. The public archive did not provide a complete raw-to-augmented crosswalk; the IYSV component itself contained 282 families but only 281 raw files. We therefore used the filename family only for within-archive splitting and made no claim that it identified a plant, plot, or biological replicate.

**Digital Green.** Twenty-four unique farmer-supplied onion photographs from the Digital Green expert-annotation collection were retained as a descriptive case series. Twelve were labelled healthy, although several notes mentioned water, nutrient, salinity, or tip-drying stress; the remaining labels included thrips, downy mildew, and single or mixed blotch annotations. These records were too few and ontologically mixed for confirmatory validation. A deterministic sample of 225 unique non-onion photographs from 73 crops was used only to test crop-level out-of-scope rejection. It does not represent unknown onion disorders.

### 2.3 Image representations and classifiers

The primary model was fixed a priori as an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet18 used as a frozen 512-dimensional feature extractor, followed by standardized L2-regularized logistic regression \cite{He2016ResNet,Deng2009ImageNet}. This modest classifier isolates representation and validation effects without fitting a high-capacity network to 1,643 development images. Its approximately 11.69 million backbone parameters and 1.814 giga-operations per image describe feature extraction, not newly trained parameters.

Three baselines were included. A prevalence model returned the TOM training prevalence. A colour-shortcut model used 189 RGB-related histogram and moment features computed from BGR, HSV, and Lab channels. A full 1,957-dimensional handcrafted representation added edge and texture summaries and histogram-of-oriented-gradient features \cite{Dalal2005HOG}. Every learned baseline used the same standardization, class weighting, regularization search, calibration, and threshold protocol as the primary model, implemented with scikit-learn \cite{Pedregosa2011ScikitLearn}.

Baseline fairness was enforced at the decision-pipeline level: each learned comparator used the same TOM folds, source-only tuning boundary, cross-fitted calibration rule, threshold objective, and locked COLD endpoint. No comparator received an additional image view, target-domain label, or target-domain selection opportunity. The backbones were kept frozen because 1,643 development images do not support a defensible comparison of several end-to-end high-capacity networks. ResNet18 was selected before the primary transfer experiment as a moderate reference representation; no superiority claim is attached to that architecture choice.

EfficientNet-B0, ConvNeXt-Tiny, and Swin-T were added after the primary analysis as architecture sensitivities. They were listed before feature extraction and retained regardless of COLD scores. Frozen ImageNet features had dimensions 1,280, 768, and 768; backbones had approximately 5.29, 28.59, and 28.29 million parameters and 0.386, 4.456, and 4.491 giga-operations per image. COLD was never used to fit, calibrate, threshold, retain, or discard them. Because the primary analysis had already opened COLD, these are post-primary sensitivities, not a second pristine confirmation.

### 2.4 Grouped nested fitting, calibration, and locked transfer

TOM2024 was divided into five stratified outer folds, with acquisition day as the grouping variable. Within each outer training partition, four stratified group folds selected the logistic-regression penalty parameter from (C\in\{10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2},10^{-1},1,10,100\}) by balanced accuracy. Standardization was fitted inside each training fold. Inner out-of-fold decision scores then fitted a one-variable logistic Platt calibrator. The threshold maximizing the inner Youden index, sensitivity minus false-positive rate, was applied to the untouched outer days. Hence every TOM prediction used a base model, calibrator, and threshold learned without that image or its acquisition day. Nested validation avoids using the same observations both to select a model and estimate its error \cite{Varma2006Bias}; day grouping aligns the split with temporal and hierarchical dependence \cite{Roberts2017CrossValidation}.

For external evaluation, each representation was refitted on all TOM images at a (C) selected by a separate five-fold, acquisition-day-grouped source-only search. A final Platt calibrator and threshold were derived from the complete TOM nested out-of-fold score stream, then frozen. That unchanged pipeline was applied to COLD and the descriptive Digital Green subsets. COLD labels therefore influenced neither the representation nor any fitted coefficient, calibration mapping, threshold, or model-retention decision.

As a post hoc calibration diagnostic, we rebuilt the original ResNet18 out-of-fold score stream using its five selected penalties (0.01, 0.01, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1), and compared it with scores obtained using the already selected final (C=0.1) in every fold. Each source score stream fitted its own Platt mapping and Youden threshold. Both were applied to the same final classifier trained on all TOM images. No COLD observation selected either construction. The original pipeline remains primary; this sensitivity checks whether mixing penalty-specific score scales materially explains its external failure. Its source calibration data are not reported as unbiased validation estimates, and the training-size change from fold models to the final refit remains in both constructions.

### 2.5 Descriptive splitting and controlled sibling exposure

The original analysis compared stratified 80/20 file and family partitions of augmented COLD over 30 seeds, using four archive labels and standardized, class-balanced logistic regression with fixed (C=0.01). These partitions differ in both evaluated images and training composition. Their score difference is a descriptive split contrast, not an isolated estimate of sibling exposure. Contamination for test set (T_r) and training set (S_r) was

\[
c_r=\frac{1}{|T_r|}\sum_{i\in T_r} \mathbb{I}\{f_i\in F(S_r)\},
\]

Equation (1) records the fraction of evaluated files with a training sibling, where (f_i) identifies a filename family and the function F gives the set of families represented in a training set. No augmentation-family identifier was used as a model feature.

To reduce the composition and budget ambiguity, a post hoc controlled replacement experiment used 30 seeds fixed as 20261002 through 20261031. Each class was partitioned into probe (P), never-exposed sentinel (S) and development (D) families. Their totals were 163, 82 and 571, respectively. Every probe or sentinel family contributed one fixed evaluation image; every development family contributed two fixed training images, giving 1,142 training files. Each probe family was paired with a same-class development donor and contributed two non-anchor siblings as replacement candidates. At nominal exposure fractions 0, 0.5 and 1, the selected donors' two training views were replaced with the paired probe family's two siblings. The middle arm exposed 81/163 probe families (49.7%). Sentinel families never entered training. Within a seed, all arms preserved the exact evaluation images, training file count, training class counts, number of training families and two-views-per-family budget. The zero-exposure arm contained no probe or sentinel family in training; the full arm exposed every probe family.

Frozen ResNet18 and colour features used the same standardized, class-balanced logistic specification at (C=0.01). Standardization was fitted separately on each arm's training files. Four-class balanced accuracy was primary; the healthy-versus-affected endpoint was a prespecified secondary analysis of the same new split schedule. For each seed and exposure (a), we compared the change on probe images with the change on sentinel images:

\[
D_r(a)=\{m_{P,r}(a)-m_{P,r}(0)\}-\{m_{S,r}(a)-m_{S,r}(0)\}.
\]

In Eq. (2), (m) is balanced accuracy. The mean and 2.5th–97.5th percentiles across seeds summarize design sensitivity, not independent plant or farm sampling. This contrast tests whether gains concentrate in exposed families after allowing for a general effect of changing training content. It is a controlled replacement effect, not an unrestricted causal estimate of all forms of leakage. Only four purple-blotch probe families and two sentinel families enter a repetition, limiting class-specific precision. All replacement assignments, predictions and invariant checks are released.

### 2.6 Outcomes and uncertainty

Balanced accuracy was primary because both sources were imbalanced. Macro-F1, sensitivity, specificity, AUROC, average precision, Brier score, negative log likelihood, and 10-bin expected calibration error (ECE) assessed decisions, ranking, and probabilities \cite{Brier1950Verification,Saito2015PrecisionRecall,Naeini2015Calibration,VanCalster2019Calibration}. TOM and COLD intervals used 1,000 acquisition-day and exact-SHA-group bootstrap replicates, respectively; transport gaps and paired COLD contrasts used 3,000 resamples. ECE is a point estimate because a nonlinear clustered interval was not used. COLD exact-SHA groups are single images, so these are image-level intervals without biological clustering. All bootstrap comparisons resample fixed predictions; they do not include retraining or full model-development uncertainty.

Selective risk was the error fraction among predictions retained after sorting by maximum binary probability. The open-set stress test compared COLD onion images with the 225 non-onion photographs using maximum probability and mean cosine distance to the five nearest TOM features \cite{Hendrycks2016OOD,Geifman2017SelectiveClassification}. These diagnostics were interpreted as shift-sensitive rather than universal uncertainty measures \cite{Ovadia2019UncertaintyShift}. OOD AUROC, false-positive rate at 95% OOD sensitivity (FPR95), and open-set classification rate were descriptive. A favourable crop-level OOD result would still not establish rejection of an unseen onion disorder.

### 2.7 Validation and reporting safeguards

The reporting checklist applies established grouped-validation principles to the available metadata. Let (g(i)) identify the source unit relevant to a stated evaluation claim. Except in the deliberately exposed probe arm, the corresponding train and test unit sets must satisfy

\[
G(S_{\mathrm{train}})\cap G(S_{\mathrm{test}})=\varnothing,
\qquad
G(S)=\{g(i):i\in S\}.
\]

Equations (3) and (4) state the overlap condition and define the unit set. For transfer, the complete representation, classifier, calibrator and threshold must be fitted without external labels. We report nominal and unique file counts; the meaning and class counts of recovered families or days; overlap checks; training and tuning boundaries; calibration and threshold data; model-selection chronology; baseline complexity; and the resampling unit. Missing plant or plot identifiers limit inference and are not replaced by assumed independence. These safeguards are a reporting aid, not an original validation algorithm or a claim that family, day and dataset form one nested experimental hierarchy.

The authors are responsible for the study design, interpretation, reference verification and final submitted content. All scientific figures were generated deterministically from machine-readable analysis outputs with the released plotting code; no generative-image system was used to create or modify them. No artificial-intelligence system is listed as an author.

## 3 Results

### 3.1 Nominal file counts overstated independent source support

The COLD augmented archive appeared large at file level but contracted sharply when represented by source families (Fig. 1). Its 4,502 files corresponded to 816 families. The contraction differed by class: healthy had three files per family, pooled leaf blight 13.5, IYSV 4.5, and purple blotch 40.8. The nominal purple-blotch count of 735 therefore rested on only 18 archive-defined families. This imbalance means that file-level class balance was created partly by unequal replication rather than equal acquisition of independent source images.

The source hierarchy also mattered in TOM2024. In a 30-repeat split-severity analysis, naive file-random partitions left a raw-photo mate in training for 30.7% of test files and shared an acquisition day for 99.4%. Raw-photo grouping removed the first overlap but still shared days for 99.3% of test files. Acquisition-day grouping removed both. Mean ResNet18 balanced accuracy was 0.939 with file-random splitting, 0.936 with raw-photo grouping, and 0.928 with day grouping. The numerical decrement was modest, but the inferential target changed from interpolation among shared collection contexts to new-day performance.

### 3.2 Controlled exposure concentrated gains in probe families

The original file-versus-family analysis found a mean test-sibling fraction of 98.5% under ordinary file splitting and zero under family grouping. ResNet18 balanced accuracy was 0.832 versus 0.675, a descriptive difference of 0.157; colour-feature values were 0.702 versus 0.437. The test images and training composition differed between schemes, so these contrasts alone do not identify how much of the difference was due to sibling exposure (Online Resource 1, Table S4).

The controlled replacement experiment held the evaluated files and training budget fixed (Fig. 2; Online Resource 1, Table S10). For four-class ResNet18 classification, full probe-family exposure increased mean probe balanced accuracy from 0.642 to 0.795, a paired gain of 0.153. Sentinel balanced accuracy changed from 0.613 to 0.605, a change of −0.009. The mean probe-minus-sentinel change was 0.162, with a 2.5th–97.5th split-seed percentile range of −0.014–0.355. Gains were therefore concentrated in the exposed probe set on average, but were not uniformly positive across partition choices. A near-zero average sentinel change is not evidence of statistical equivalence.

The mean full-exposure probe recall gain for ResNet18 was 0.042 for healthy, 0.079 for IYSV, 0.308 for purple blotch and 0.181 for pooled leaf blight. The largest observed gain occurred in the class with only 18 source families; this is an archive-specific association, not evidence that this disorder is intrinsically more susceptible to leakage.

The colour comparator showed a larger probe increase, from 0.417 to 0.701 (+0.284), alongside a sentinel change of +0.002. Its mean probe-minus-sentinel change was 0.282 (seed percentile range 0.086–0.506). This dependence-sensitive gain did not require a deep representation. It indicates that sibling familiarity can strengthen simple appearance cues; the experiment does not identify particular pixels or prove which visual cue the classifier used.

Endpoint choice materially changed the result. On the same family assignments, binary healthy-versus-affected ResNet18 probe balanced accuracy increased by 0.038, with sentinel change +0.002 and probe-minus-sentinel change 0.036 (−0.024–0.103). The binary colour contrast was 0.089 (0.014–0.179). The four-class leakage magnitude therefore cannot be transferred to the binary source-shift analysis. The experiment retained all three doses and both representations, including non-monotonic or small individual-seed effects. Class recalls and the complete paired prediction stream are released; purple-blotch inference remains limited by four probe and two sentinel families per seed.

### 3.3 The a-priori model transported poorly despite strong internal performance

Table 2 summarizes the full model panel. Acquisition-day-grouped TOM validation gave the primary ResNet18 model balanced accuracy 0.927 (95% interval 0.909–0.945), macro-F1 0.904 (0.875–0.928), AUROC 0.979 (0.970–0.986), Brier score 0.049 (0.038–0.062), and ECE 0.021. With the TOM-derived calibrator and threshold locked, COLD balanced accuracy was 0.655 (0.626–0.685), macro-F1 0.645 (0.610–0.677), and AUROC 0.735 (0.700–0.770). The balanced-accuracy transport gap was -0.272 (3,000-resample interval -0.306 to -0.235). The Brier score worsened to 0.240 (0.218–0.262), a gap of 0.191 (0.165–0.216), while ECE rose to 0.168 (Fig. 3).

The external result exceeded the prespecified simple baselines without approaching the internal estimate. COLD balanced accuracy was 0.500 for prevalence, 0.525 for the colour shortcut, and 0.571 for the full handcrafted model. ResNet18 improved on the strongest of these by 0.085 (paired interval 0.045–0.124). Its AUROC advantage over the handcrafted model was 0.132 (0.093–0.171), and its Brier score was lower by 0.048 (0.024–0.072). Thus, failure to transport cannot be reduced to a simple baseline beating the primary model; it is the gap between credible within-source performance and only moderate external reliability.

At the locked threshold of 0.815, ResNet18 sensitivity on COLD was 0.444 and specificity was 0.866. Correct fractions varied across COLD labels: 369/426 healthy images (0.866), 115/279 IYSV images (0.412), 51/90 pooled leaf-blight images (0.567), and 6/18 purple-blotch images (0.333). These are errors against dataset labels. They are not sensitivity estimates for clinically or laboratory-confirmed pathogens.

Reconstructing the original calibration pipeline in the current numerical environment reproduced all 813 external decisions and changed probabilities by less than 3 × 10⁻¹⁶. Using the final (C=0.1) consistently for source out-of-fold calibration changed external balanced accuracy from 0.655 to 0.646 (paired difference −0.009; 1,000-image-bootstrap interval −0.022–0.003) and Brier score from 0.240 to 0.231 (difference −0.009; −0.011–−0.007). This modest probability improvement did not restore external decision performance (Online Resource 1, Table S11). AUROC was identical because both positive-slope calibrators transformed the same final raw scores; this equality is mathematical, not an additional replication of discrimination.

### 3.4 Newer backbones changed rankings, not the reliability conclusion

All three post-primary challengers scored highly in TOM grouped validation: balanced accuracy was 0.929 for EfficientNet-B0, 0.939 for ConvNeXt-Tiny, and 0.953 for Swin-T. Their locked COLD balanced accuracies were 0.650, 0.583, and 0.562, respectively. Every architecture therefore showed a material transport gap, ranging from -0.279 for EfficientNet-B0 to -0.390 for Swin-T.

Swin-T is the clearest illustration of why ranking, calibration, and operating-point transfer must be separated. It had the highest COLD AUROC, 0.782 (0.751–0.811), exceeding ResNet18 by 0.048 in a paired comparison. Yet its TOM-locked threshold yielded sensitivity 0.127 and specificity 0.998, so balanced accuracy was only 0.562. Its COLD Brier score was 0.282 and ECE 0.286, versus 0.240 and 0.168 for ResNet18. Swin-T retained useful ordering information but shifted its probability scale and operating point so strongly that nearly all affected COLD images fell below the source-derived decision threshold. Calling it the “best” model would depend on silently replacing the prespecified decision objective with external AUROC after seeing the target labels. We therefore retain ResNet18 as the sole primary model and report Swin-T as evidence that a strong representation does not guarantee threshold or calibration transport.

### 3.5 Abstention and crop-level OOD scores did not support deployment

For ResNet18 on COLD, error risk decreased from 0.335 at full coverage to 0.162 at 25% coverage (Fig. 4). This improvement is informative, but one error among roughly six accepted predictions at the most selective reported point is not adequate for an autonomous diagnostic claim. Swin-T, despite its higher external AUROC, had risk 0.196 at 25% coverage and 0.417 at full coverage, consistent with its poorly transported threshold.

Using source-feature five-nearest-neighbour distance, ResNet18 separated COLD onion images from Digital Green non-onion images with OOD AUROC 0.795, but FPR95 was 0.873 and the open-set classification rate was 0.532 (Online Resource 1, Fig. S1). Swin-T achieved OOD AUROC 0.916, yet FPR95 remained 0.399. These results concern other crop species, a relatively easy and different unknown class. They cannot be interpreted as detection of a novel onion disease. The 24-image Digital Green onion case series was likewise retained only to expose annotation and context heterogeneity, not to supply another confirmatory performance estimate.

## 4 Discussion

Two different reliability questions require different controls. The augmentation experiment asks whether gains are concentrated in test families whose siblings enter training while the evaluated images and training budget remain fixed. The transfer experiment asks whether a source-developed decision rule remains useful on another archive. These contrasts have distinct tasks and sampling units. Together they distinguish a dependence-related evaluation advantage from a failure of probabilities and decisions after source change; they do not establish one universal ordering of validation difficulty.

The controlled comparison addresses a limitation of ordinary split comparisons: changing the split unit also changes which observations are evaluated and how many related training examples remain. Gerz and Jelali demonstrated agricultural augmentation leakage, while Apicella et al. studied contamination proportions and Guignard et al. derived leakage properties under clustering \cite{Gerz2026AugmentationLeakage,Apicella2025Leakage,Guignard2024ClusterLeakage}. Neither the existence of leakage nor the idea of varying its dose is new here. Our empirical contribution is the combination of non-identical siblings from an existing archive, identical probe and sentinel images across arms, fixed class and family budgets, and complete replacement records. The sentinel contrast matters because replacing training families can change overall model quality even without test-family familiarity. Filename lineage also exposed unequal support that content uniqueness missed: 735 purple-blotch files represented only 18 families. Such provenance should be preserved alongside raw images and augmentation code.

The day-grouped and external results implement recommendations that have often remained aspirational. Xu et al. called for challenge-oriented test sets and spatial-temporal metadata because random partitions do not represent the conditions encountered after deployment \cite{Xu2024PlantDiseaseDatasets}. Here, acquisition day was recoverable in TOM2024 and was used in every inner and outer fold. COLD lacked plant and day identifiers, which prevented an equivalent biological or temporal analysis. This asymmetry itself is a result: without acquisition metadata, neither a complex model nor a more elaborate confidence interval can recover the missing unit of replication.

Our external findings accord with recent reliability-centred plant-imaging studies while adding a distinct lineage component. Workneh et al. reported high correlation-aware internal macro-F1 for almond images but a much lower result on a source-disjoint stress test, alongside calibration and selective-prediction analysis \cite{Workneh2027AlmondReliability}. Xiang et al. found a 67.7-percentage-point accuracy collapse from controlled PlantVillage imagery to PlantDoc, with calibration, abstention, adaptation, and ensembles unable to establish deployment-grade reliability \cite{Xiang2026ReliabilityGap}. We likewise observed a 27.2-point balanced-accuracy decline and a fivefold increase in Brier score for the primary onion model. The studies differ in crop, endpoint, domains, and metrics, so their magnitudes should not be pooled. Their common lesson is that source-disjoint evaluation changes the scientific conclusion more than small in-domain architectural gains.

The Swin-T sensitivity sharpens that lesson. Its external AUROC showed that ImageNet-pretrained transformer features preserved more pairwise ordering on COLD than the primary ResNet18 features. However, the probabilities produced after TOM-only calibration were shifted, and the source-selected threshold was too conservative for external affected images. The result is not paradoxical: AUROC is invariant to monotonic score transformations, whereas balanced accuracy at a fixed threshold and the Brier score depend on the transported probability scale and operating point. Recalibrating or re-thresholding on COLD could change target-fitted performance, but would answer a different question, consume target labels and require a separate target validation set to estimate any improvement. For a locked transfer claim, the failure must remain visible.

The relationship to recent PAA work is methodological rather than metric-by-metric (Table 3). Menco-Tovar et al. are the direct leakage-control precedent: they kept all ROIs from one microscopy image within one subset and contrasted that design with ROI-level partitioning \cite{MencoTovar2026OriginalImage}. Their study establishes the core two-level principle and quantifies a smaller, task-specific inflation. The present study adds fixed-test, fixed-budget sibling replacement with a never-exposed sentinel set and a distinct source-locked calibration and threshold analysis. Menco-Tovar et al. had stronger expert review of biological labels; our folder-label endpoint cannot match that evidence. Conversely, our external analysis addresses a source change that their study identified as future work. Neither distinction establishes superiority across unlike tasks. Kurcuma makes multiple acquisition domains explicit and supplies a benchmark for unsupervised adaptation \cite{Rosello2023Kurcuma}. The correlation-alignment/entropy and masked-adversarial studies propose ways to learn across unlabeled target domains \cite{Gilo2024Subdomain,Chouhan2025MDAAF}; the fruit-disease GAN study addresses imbalance in an agricultural recognition task \cite{Gupta2025FruitGAN}. Mitchell et al. address spurious-correlation robustness, and Gebler et al. provide a recent agricultural dataset-and-segmentation evaluation \cite{Mitchell2025Spurious,Gebler2026StereoInsect}. Those contributions concern datasets, mitigation, or learning mechanisms. Our controls concern the conditions under which a reported score can be interpreted before adaptation. The questions are complementary: an adaptation or mitigation method should still be evaluated with source units separated, tuning confined to its declared information regime, and final calibration and operating-point claims tested without silent target-label reuse.

The baseline results reduce another source of ambiguity. Colour and handcrafted models performed above chance internally, confirming that low-level acquisition and appearance cues were predictive in TOM2024. ResNet18 nevertheless beat those baselines on locked COLD balanced accuracy, AUROC, and Brier score. The external weakness therefore cannot be dismissed as a need to replace a needlessly complex model with a simple classifier. Conversely, the challengers show that increasing representational capacity does not solve the problem automatically. The 28-million-parameter ConvNeXt-Tiny and Swin-T models achieved higher internal balanced accuracy than ResNet18 but worse external thresholded performance. With only 1,643 development images, freezing generic features and fitting a regularized linear head was an intentional control against excessive end-to-end capacity; the result points to data diversity and metadata, not a missing optimization trick.

For dataset builders, raw sources should be partitioned before augmentation. Parent, plant, plot, farm, camera, date, and annotator identifiers should be preserved when possible. Independent tests should match the stated generalization claim, and labels should record how a disorder was established. Reports should show nominal files and relevant source-unit counts, then reassess calibration after source shift.

For model developers, file checks are useful for debugging; family- or specimen-grouped splits test beyond siblings; day-, plot-, or farm-grouped validation probes contextual dependence; and a locked external dataset tests combined acquisition and case-mix change. Each design supports its stated generalization target; success under one design does not establish success under another.

## 5 Limitations

Several limits define the interpretation. First, the endpoint is based on dataset-assigned visual status. No common pathology protocol was applied across TOM2024 and COLD, and we did not relabel images; suspected ontology or label-quality problems were treated as audit limits rather than silently corrected targets \cite{Northcutt2021ConfidentLearning}. The analysis cannot estimate pathogen-specific sensitivity, distinguish biotic from all abiotic symptoms, or recommend management.

Second, the external contrast is bundled. TOM2024 and COLD differ jointly in dataset construction, country, camera and processing pipeline, background, disease mix, prevalence, and label practice. These variables are confounded by source. The observed gap is a cross-dataset transport gap and cannot be attributed independently to India, Burkina Faso, a camera type, or any other single factor.

Third, neither archive supplied plant, plot, or farm identifiers suitable for biological clustering. TOM acquisition day is a conservative contextual group, not a biological replicate. COLD provided neither day nor plant identity, so its bootstrap unit was the exact-deduplicated raw file. Those intervals quantify image-level uncertainty under the available metadata and may still be narrower than plant- or farm-level intervals.

Fourth, augmentation families came from a complete filename pattern, but the raw-parent crosswalk was incomplete. We therefore kept raw COLD external validation and fixed-augmented leakage analysis separate. The family experiment establishes dependency among transformed files; it does not establish how many independent plants generated the raw archive.

Fifth, the architecture sensitivities were specified and run after COLD had been opened for the primary ResNet18 analysis. Their pipelines remained target-label independent, but they are robustness analyses rather than a new external confirmation. A future prospective study should freeze all architectures before acquiring a new external cohort.

The controlled replacement and calibration diagnostics were added after the original findings. Freezing their own protocols before execution prevents result-driven changes within these analyses, but does not turn them into prospective confirmations. Probe and sentinel families are randomized within class, yet replacing training content does not isolate every possible source of dependence. The purple-blotch class contains only 18 families in total. A second archive with a known parent mapping and a prospective acquisition would test whether the controlled effect recurs beyond COLD.

Finally, Digital Green contributes only 24 onion images and its healthy annotations include textual stress observations. It is a descriptive case series. The 225-image OOD subset contains non-onion crops only, so its OOD metrics measure crop-level novelty rather than unknown onion symptoms. Neither analysis supports deployment or open-world onion diagnosis.

## 6 Conclusion

In a public onion archive, exposing fixed test families' siblings while holding training budgets constant produced a mean four-class probe gain of 0.153 for ResNet18, with little average change on an unexposed sentinel set. The smaller binary gain and wide seed variability show that this effect depends on the endpoint and partition. Separately, a source-developed classifier retained only moderate external decision performance despite strong grouped internal validation. A calibration-construction sensitivity improved probability error modestly without removing that failure. These findings support provenance-aware experimental controls and separate assessment of ranking, calibration and frozen decisions. They do not establish pathogen diagnosis, biological replication, or deployment readiness. Repeating the controlled design in another lineage-traceable archive and testing a prospectively locked pipeline on independently acquired plants remain necessary extensions.
